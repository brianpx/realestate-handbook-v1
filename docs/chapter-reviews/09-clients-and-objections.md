# Chapter 9. Working with clients and dealing with objections

**Verdict.** The five-step consultation (Table 9.1) and the four-step objection pattern (acknowledge, clarify, respond, check) are the right tools, and the idea that objections are questions in disguise is the right frame. What the chapter does not do is *model* them. The four-step pattern is stated, then the response table gives one-line frameworks that do not visibly follow it. There is no annotated conversation, no sample note, and no service content between "agree" and "after the transaction", though the title says "working with clients".

## Title vs. content

"Working with clients" suggests the whole service relationship: showings, offers, negotiation, communication during the transaction. The chapter covers the **consultation**, **objections**, **documentation** and a closing paragraph about the post-transaction. That is a sensible scope for this handbook (finding, nurturing, keeping), but the title overstates it.

Retitle to **"Consultations and objections"** or keep the title and add a short section, "Between yes and closing", on communication cadence during a transaction (weekly update, a call before any deadline, one named point of contact). A client who is well informed becomes a past client who refers. That section connects Chapter 9 to Chapter 10 directly and is the natural home of the paragraph currently titled "After the transaction".

Rewrite:

> In this chapter you will learn to:
> - **Lead** a buyer or seller consultation through five steps, spending at least half the meeting asking and listening.
> - **Answer** an objection using four steps (acknowledge, clarify, respond, check), and **identify** which step each line of a sample answer performs.
> - **Write** a two-minute CRM note after a client conversation: date, who, what was discussed, what was decided, what happens next.

## Opening and transition

Part intro: "A consultation is a conversation. An objection is a request for information. A closed client is the beginning of the relationship, not the end." It works as the chapter's thesis, three sentences that match the three sections. The chapter lede repeats the first one. Remove the repetition.

The bridge from Chapter 8 is absent. Chapters 1–8 are about getting appointments; this is the first chapter about what happens in one.

**Replacement opener:**

> Everything so far has been about getting a person to the table: finding them, following up, staying organized enough to show up. This chapter is about the meeting itself.
>
> A good consultation is a conversation, not a pitch. You ask, you listen, and only then do you show how you work. When the client pushes back, that is rarely a refusal. It is usually a question they have not yet put into words. You will learn a five-step shape for the meeting, a four-step pattern for the pushback, and the two-minute habit that protects both of you afterward.

**Add a worked example** (the biggest gap). Annotate one objection response, step by step:

> **Objection:** "Your fee is too high."
> 1. *Acknowledge:* "I understand. It's a big number." (heard)
> 2. *Clarify:* "Can I ask what you're comparing it to?" (find the real concern: another agent's quote, a net figure, or a general worry)
> 3. *Respond:* walk through what they would net and what the work involves. (answer the real concern)
> 4. *Check:* "Does that make sense?" Then stop.

At present Table 9.2 gives *frameworks* in a single line, and the four steps are not marked. A reader cannot tell where step 3 begins. Mark each response with the steps it uses.

**Sample CRM note** (objective 3 has none):

> *14 Oct, Priya & Dev Shah, buyer consult, 55 min. Goals: move by Aug for school; budget $520k; pre-approved with [lender]. Concern: "we may wait for rates." Discussed: cost of waiting vs. buying now. Decided: agreement to be signed Thursday; send three homes by Wednesday. Next: call Thursday 5 pm.*

That is five lines and is what the "Document everything" section describes in words.

**The spoken test** in the interview-other-agents script box is excellent. Use it as the model for step 3 in the worked example.

## Prose and accuracy

- **"Compensation is negotiable and is no longer an MLS field."** This statement appears in the chapter body and in Table 9.2. It is directly relevant, but it is a legal claim given inside a sales script. The script says "I'll confirm the structure with my broker", which is the right instinct, but a script that puts a legal claim in the agent's mouth invites risk. Keep the box that defers to Chapter 11 and to the broker; trim the factual assertion from the spoken response, or make the response "Compensation is something we agree in writing before we start. Let me show you how I work and what you'd net." See [11](11-legal-and-professional-standards.md).
- **Table 9.2, "You're new, aren't you?"** is honest and a good counterweight to the Chapter 6 referral script that overstates experience. Cross-link.
- **"Agents who skip the asking and go straight to presenting lose the client's trust before they have earned it."** The best sentence in the chapter. Keep.
- **Table 9.2 caption** says "These are the objections you will hear most in year one", which is a claim, not a finding. Soften to "These are common objections."
- **"Steps 2 and 3 should take at least half the meeting."** Right, and it is the most testable claim in the book. Use Figure 9.1 to make it visible.
- **The consultation widget** ("Rebalance a meeting so asking and listening take half") practices exactly this. Reference it in the chapter text as well as in Appendix E.
- **Duplicate:** "I'm just looking" appears in Chapter 6's script and as row 1 of Table 9.2. See [06](06-scripts.md).
