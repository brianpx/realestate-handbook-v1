# Chapter 5. Nurturing leads

**Verdict.** The best-taught chapter in the book. It opens with a problem (one call is never enough), supplies a concrete 13-touch model, states design properties, then teaches qualifying and a weekly review, each with a table and a clear action. What holds it back is a plan library whose names collide (three new-lead plans, one of them missing from the table), a figure that is a numbered list, and exercises that assume the reader already has leads, which a new agent in the first month does not.

## Title vs. content

"Nurturing leads" is exactly what is taught: why multiple touches, how to design a plan, which plan fits whom, how to qualify, how to review. Part IV also includes Chapter 6 (scripts), which belongs to Nurture only loosely; scripts serve Find and Keep too. See [06](06-scripts.md).

The chapter has four substantial ideas. A reader can lose one. Consider a one-sentence roadmap at the top: *"Four ideas: why you follow up, what a good plan looks like, how to qualify, and how to check you are doing it."*

## Opening and transition

The part intro is the best in the book ("Most leads convert between the fifth and twelfth touch. Most agents quit after the second. The gap between those two facts is where your business is."). It deserves to be the chapter's first paragraph, not an italic aside, and it needs the source note (README #7). Chapter 4 ended with speed-to-lead and a first automated touch; Chapter 5 should pick up from there.

**Replacement opener:**

> The first minute is the easy part. Your CRM sends a text, you pick up the phone, and, if you are quick, you reach someone. Then what? Most leads are not ready to decide on the first call. They are curious, early, unsure of financing, or waiting on a life event.
>
> Most leads convert between the fifth and twelfth touch. Most agents quit after the second. The gap between those two facts is where your business is. This chapter shows you how to cross it: a 90-day plan that rotates calls, texts and emails; four questions that tell you how to help; and one Friday habit that keeps anyone from slipping away.

## Prose and accuracy

- **"Industry follow-up data consistently shows that the majority of conversions happen between the fifth and twelfth contact."** The anchor claim of the chapter, with no source. A busy reader, a broker or a skeptic will ask. Cite or soften to "many agents report". See README #7.
- **"A single channel reaches a third of your list."** Unsupported and oddly precise. Replace with "No single channel reaches everyone."
- **Duplicated paragraph.** "Get permission before an automated marketing text, honor quiet hours, and stop on the first opt-out. An inquiry is not, by itself, permission for a text sequence" appears here, in Chapter 4 and in Appendix B.1, word for word. Repeating a safety rule is good practice; verbatim repetition reads as copy-paste. Vary the wording or box the rule once and link to it.
- **"Seven touches in the first 14 days."** Correct against Figure 5 (touches 1–7). Nice precision.
- **The Friday review (three questions)** is excellent: short, memorable, answerable. Keep unchanged.
- **Pitfall box** ("Treating 'no answer' as 'not interested'") is a very good fit for the chapter's thesis. Move it next to the "Why one call is never enough" section, where the lesson lands first.
- **Field Note** ("Hi Jordan, it's Sam with [Brokerage]…") introduces a named example text. Use "Sam" as the recurring case agent across the book. See README #3.
- **Chapter length**: at about 12.8 KB this is the longest chapter. If it is split, the natural seam is "plans" (Why one call is never enough → starter library) and "qualify and review" (Qualify early → pipeline review). Not required, but a mid-chapter recap line ("So far: you know why, and how to build the plan. Next: who to prioritize") would help.
