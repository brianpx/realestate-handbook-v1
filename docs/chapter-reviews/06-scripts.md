# Chapter 6. Scripts: conversations that convert

**Verdict.** The "how to use a script" section is good teaching: rewrite, say aloud, record, listen. The script boxes are well formed because each states a purpose and a next step. The problems are of selection and honesty. The scripts the rest of the book asks the reader to use are not here (the sphere announcement, voicemail, open-house greeting). One script has the new agent claiming experience they do not have. Two scripts repeat in Chapter 9. And the chapter says what a good script does but never shows one being taken apart.

Rewrite:

> In this chapter you will learn to:
> - **Open** a conversation with a new lead by phone, text and voicemail, naming what they asked about.
> - **Announce** your new career to your sphere, and **ask** for introductions in a way that gives before it asks.
> - **Apply** three rules (be specific, ask one question, stop talking) to any message you write.
> - **Rehearse** a script out loud until you can say it in your own words.

## Opening and transition

There is no part intro. The lede ("Scripts are starting points, not lines to recite") is correct and defensive. Chapter 5 ended on a Friday review habit. Chapter 6 should connect to what the reader has just built: plans full of calls and texts they now have to *say*.

**Replacement opener:**

> Your follow-up plan now says "Day 7: call." That is a task, not a conversation. Many new agents see the task, feel the pause before the call, and push it to tomorrow.
>
> A script gets you past the pause. It is not something to read; it is a way to know the first sentence, the one question, and what "done" looks like. This chapter gives you the scripts that your follow-up plans, your sphere and your first consultations will call for, and a method to make them sound like you.

## Exercises

| Try It | Relevance | Builds on earlier? | Issue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rewrite the new-lead phone script; record yourself three times | High | Uses the Ch. 4 speed-to-lead call | Excellent. Add a rubric (below). |
| Role-play the buyer consultation opening with a colleague; swap roles | High | Previews Ch. 9 | Good. Give the partner a checklist so feedback is specific. |
| Use the sphere referral ask on three calls this week; log the outcomes | High | Uses Ch. 2 sphere, Ch. 4 CRM | Good, but see the credibility issue: a new agent should not ask for referrals before they have given anything (Ch. 2 order: announce, give, ask). |

**Add a self-review rubric** (a three-item card the reader uses on the recording):

> Did I (1) use their name and the thing they asked about, (2) end on one question, (3) stop talking after it? Mark yes/no for each recording. Aim for three yes.

This turns "listen back" into measurable feedback and ties the exercise to the three rules.

**Make it cumulative.** In Try It 1, add: "Use the lead from your Chapter 5 speed-to-lead test as the person you are calling." In Try It 3, add: "Choose three people from your Chapter 2 sphere list who have already received a 'give' touch."

## Diagrams

No figure today. One would make the chapter's central idea concrete: every script has the same four parts. A reader can then build their own.

*Prompt: Figure 6.1, anatomy of a script.*

> [Shared style from README.] Title: "Figure 6.1. Every script has four parts." Show the new-lead phone script as a stacked card of four horizontal bands, each with a colored left tab and a label: **1 Name them** ("Hi [Name], this is [Your Name] with [Brokerage]."), **2 Show you read it** ("I just saw your inquiry about [address or area]."), **3 Ask one question** ("Do you have a couple of minutes so I can answer your questions?"), **4 Stop** (an empty band with a small clock icon-free label "Wait. Let them answer."). To the right, a column labelled "Why" with one short line per band: "People answer to their name", "Specific proves a human read it", "A message with no question gets no reply", "Silence is where they decide to engage." Caption: "Take any script apart and you will find these four."

A second image is optional: a **practice loop** (read → rewrite → say aloud → record → listen → repeat) as a simple circle. The method is in one paragraph and is the most transferable idea in the chapter.

## Prose and accuracy

- **Sphere referral script: "I've been working with several families in the area…"** A new agent in the first 90 days has not been working with several families, and may have none. The script asks them to say something untrue. Chapter 9 teaches honesty ("You're new, aren't you?" → "I am, and that means you get my full attention"). The two chapters conflict. Replace with a version that is true on day one: *"I'm building my business on introductions from people I trust, and I'd be grateful if you thought of me when someone you know mentions buying or selling. No pressure."*
- **"Either is a win. Thank them and move on."** Good, but "move on" reads as dismissal. "Thank them and keep in touch" matches the book's philosophy.
- **"A message with no question gets no reply."** Memorable and plausible, but absolute. "A message with no question rarely gets a reply."
- **Three rules** ("Say their name and the specific thing… Ask one question… Stop talking.") are clear and the strongest content in the chapter. Put them *before* the scripts, so the reader can use them to read each script. Today they arrive after.
- **Script voice.** The scripts are warm and plain. Check "Sound good?" in the buyer opening for regional tone. It is the right idea (ask for agreement), but a lighter "Does that work for you?" is more widely natural.
- **Compliance.** The new-lead text script is sent to someone who inquired. See README #5: consent is needed for automated texts. State in the script box that it is for a personal, non-automated text unless consent is recorded.
- **"I just saw your inquiry"** is true only if the call is made right after the inquiry. If the call is Day 7, the script is false. Note: *"If you are calling later, use 'following up on your inquiry'."*
