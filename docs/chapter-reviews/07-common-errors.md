# Chapter 7. Common errors and pitfalls of new agents

**Verdict.** A well-built diagnostic chapter: six errors, what each looks like, what it costs, and where the book fixes it. The loop explanation ("how the errors feed each other") is the chapter's best idea. As teaching, though, it is a recap that arrives before most of its fixes: three of the six "Fix" cells point to chapters the reader has not reached (8, 11, 12). It states a fix in the abstract but never shows an agent falling into the cycle. And "Fix: Ch. 11" for under-documenting sends the reader to a chapter that lists what to document, not how to build the habit.

## Title vs. content

The title promises *common errors and pitfalls*; the content delivers six and a systemic explanation. One omission is worth checking: **buying leads before the system is ready** is covered in Chapters 2–3 as a pitfall box and is named in the table only as "Relying only on portal leads". Those are different errors (one is over-reliance on a channel, the other is premature spend). A newcomer will recognize the second more often. Either add a seventh row or rename row 2 to "Paying for leads before the system is ready, or relying only on portals."

Also note the chapter is a **review chapter in a book that has not yet introduced half the fixes.** That is not wrong, but it changes its job: it should be the **mid-course diagnostic**, the moment the reader checks themselves against what they have learned and names the one thing to work on. The title could say that: "Halfway through: where new agents go wrong."

## Opening and transition

The part intro ("The traps are predictable, which means they are avoidable…") is good. It sits in italics where few readers pause. The chapter lede ("New agents fall into a small number of predictable traps") is accurate but flat. Chapter 6 ended with practice; Chapter 7 is where the reader steps back.

**Replacement opener:**

> You have now met the core of the system: find people, follow up, say the right words. Before the book turns to routines and conversations with clients, take a day to look at where this fails in real life.
>
> New agents fall into the same six traps. They reinforce one another, which is why a bad month can become a bad year. The good news is that the correction is the same for all six, and you already have most of it. This chapter helps you see which trap is yours.

**Add a case study** (the chapter's missing worked example). Use "Sam" from Chapter 5:

> *Sam closed no deals in the first 30 days. This week: Monday a lead came in at 4 p.m. and Sam called at 6 p.m., two hours late. Tuesday Sam spent the morning on a logo. Wednesday's follow-up tasks went undone because "nothing is due." Thursday Sam had no appointment and felt low. Friday Sam did no pipeline review. Which of the six errors can you find? In what order did they occur? Which one single change on Monday would have broken the chain?*

A case turns the table into a skill: the reader practices diagnosing before diagnosing themselves, which is easier and less defensive. It also supplies the spaced retrieval of Chapters 4–6.

**Checkpoint 2** ("In what order do the errors feed each other?") is recall of an arrow chain. Replace with: *"Break the cycle at a different link than follow-up. What happens?"* This tests understanding.

## Prose and accuracy

- **"Most of these pitfalls disappear within a month of the system being in place."** An overpromise. A reader who still struggles at day 31 may conclude the system failed. Use "Most of these pitfalls shrink quickly once the system is in place" and add "Expect to catch yourself in them, not to never fall into them."
- **"Not because you became a different person, but because the system does the remembering."** A lovely sentence; the heart of the chapter. Place it near the top, not the end.
- **"The loop runs on its own once it starts."** Memorable. Make sure the figure carries it.
- **Table 7.1 column "What it costs"** is the strongest teaching device: each cost is concrete ("quitting at the exact point the pipeline would have paid"). Row 5 ("Under-documenting") has the weakest cost ("Risk, disputes, and forgotten details"). Be specific: "A client says 'you told me the fee was negotiable.' You have nothing in writing."
- **The six-error list** mixes behaviors (slow follow-up, unstructured days) with beliefs (expecting results too fast) and a choice (portal-only). Naming them all as *errors* is fine, but the Fix column treats them uniformly. Note that "Expecting results too fast" is fixed by *information* (the 90-day timeline), not a habit. A one-line difference is worth stating.
- **"Ask your office productivity coach"** is brokerage-specific. See README #8.
- **Cross-chapter links** "(Ch. 4)" in the table are good practice. Keep, and make them live links on the site.
