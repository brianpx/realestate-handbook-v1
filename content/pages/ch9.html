PART VI: Serve and Keep

A consultation is a conversation. An objection is a request for information. A closed client is the beginning of the relationship, not the end.

9. Working with clients and dealing with objections

Everything so far has been about getting a person to the table: finding them, following up, staying organized enough to show up. This chapter is about the meeting itself.

A good consultation is a conversation, not a pitch. You ask, you listen, and only then do you show how you work. When the client pushes back, that is rarely a refusal. It is usually a question they have not yet put into words. You will learn a five-step shape for the meeting, a four-step pattern for the pushback, and the two-minute habit that protects both of you afterward.

In this chapter you will learn to

  • Lead a buyer or seller consultation through five steps, spending at least half the meeting asking and listening.
  • Answer an objection using four steps (acknowledge, clarify, respond, check), and identify which step each line of a sample answer performs.
  • Write a two-minute CRM note after a client conversation: date, who, what was discussed, what was decided, what happens next.

The consultation sequence

Both buyer and seller consultations follow the same shape. Set the frame, ask, listen, present, agree on next steps. Agents who skip the asking and go straight to presenting lose the client's trust before they have earned it.

Objections are questions in disguise

Most objections are requests for more information or reassurance. "Your fee is too high" usually means "show me what I get for it." Compensation is something you agree in writing before you start. Chapter 11 covers the rules, and your broker confirms the wording. "We want to wait" usually means "we are nervous about the market." Respond with curiosity, not defense. The four-step pattern below works for nearly every objection, and Table 9.2 applies it to the ones you will hear most.

  1. Acknowledge. "That makes sense." You are not agreeing; you are showing you heard.
  2. Clarify. "Can I ask what's behind that?" Find the real concern.
  3. Respond. Answer the real concern with facts, a story, or a demonstration.
  4. Check. "Does that address it?" Then stop talking.

Here is the pattern on the most common objection, with each step marked:

  1. Acknowledge. "I understand. It's a big number." (They know you heard.)
  2. Clarify. "Can I ask what you're comparing it to?" (Find the real concern: another agent's quote, a net figure, or a general worry.)
  3. Respond. Walk through what they would net and what the work involves. (Answer the real concern, not the one you assumed.)
  4. Check. "Does that make sense?" Then stop.

"I think that's smart; this is a big decision. Can I ask what you'll be comparing? ... Great. Then let me make sure you leave today with those answers from me, so you have a fair comparison. And if you'd like, I'll follow up Thursday after you've met the others."

Objection quick reference

Treat each response as a starting framework, not a line to memorize. After you respond, ask a question that moves the conversation forward. Log the objection and your response in your CRM so the next follow-up is specific. Practice the three or four you hear most until they feel natural.

Document everything

After every consultation, offer discussion, price change or disclosure conversation, write a short note in your CRM: date, who, what was discussed, what was decided, what happens next. Two minutes of typing protects you from "but you said" months later and makes your follow-up specific. Chapter 11 covers what to document for legal protection.

A good note is five lines or fewer. For example: 14 Oct, Priya & Dev Shah, buyer consult, 55 min. Goals: move by August for school; budget $520k; pre-approved. Concern: "we may wait for rates." Discussed: cost of waiting vs. buying now. Decided: agreement to be signed Thursday; send three homes by Wednesday. Next: call Thursday 5 p.m.

Between yes and closing

A client who is well informed becomes a past client who refers. Between the signed agreement and the closing table, give every client one named point of contact (you), a short update every week even when nothing has changed, and a call before every deadline. Log each of those in your CRM.

After the transaction

Stay in touch. The clients who feel cared for after closing become your best source of future business. Chapter 10 shows the post-close plan. The habit starts at the closing table: thank them, ask for the introduction, and tell them to expect to hear from you.

KEY TAKEAWAYS

  1. Frame, ask, listen, present, agree. Asking and listening come before presenting.
  2. Objections are requests for information. Acknowledge, clarify, respond, check.
  3. Document every conversation in your CRM the same day.
