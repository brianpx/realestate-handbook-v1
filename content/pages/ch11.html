PART VI: Serve and Keep

11. Legal protection and professional standards

Every touch you have learned so far, the text, the call, the post, the open house, sits inside a set of rules. Most of them protect the person on the other end. A few protect you. Learn them once, early, and they become habits you do not notice.

This chapter is not legal advice. Your state and your broker set the rules, and they change. What it gives you is the shape of them: what to disclose and when, how to talk about a neighborhood so that everyone is treated the same, and what to write down so that you and your clients are protected if memory fails. When in doubt, ask your broker or office leadership before you act.

In this chapter you will learn to

  • Explain agency in two sentences and deliver your state's disclosure at the first substantive contact.
  • Rewrite a listing description or social post so it describes the property, not the people.
  • Send automated texts only with consent, and stop at the first opt-out.
  • Record the seven items on the documentation list the day they happen.
● READ THIS FIRST
This chapter describes professional habits, not legal advice. Agency, disclosure, compensation and advertising rules differ by state and change over time. Your broker, your office leadership and your state association are the authorities. When any situation feels unusual, stop and ask before you act.

Agency disclosure

Before you provide services to a buyer or seller, they must understand whom you represent and what that means. Your state has a required disclosure form and a required timing, usually at first substantive contact. Learn both in your first week. Present the form in plain language, answer questions, and keep the signed copy in your transaction system. If a conversation moves from casual to substantive, that is the moment to disclose.

Buyer agreements and compensation

Two rules a new agent in 2026 will be corrected on in week one. Confirm both with your broker before you use them:

  • Get a written buyer representation agreement before you tour homes. Your state and your broker set when it has to be signed.
  • Compensation is negotiable. An offer of compensation to the buyer's agent is no longer a field in the MLS. Who pays, and how much, is a conversation with your client. Do not quote a number from an old listing form.

This is a pointer, not legal advice. Your broker has the current form and the words to use.

Fair Housing

Federal law prohibits discrimination in housing on the basis of race, color, religion, national origin, sex, familial status and disability. Many states and cities add further protected classes. Ask your broker for yours. The rules apply to how you advertise, whom you serve, what you say about neighborhoods and how you treat every inquiry.

  • In marketing. Describe the property, not the ideal buyer. "Great for families" and "perfect for young professionals" are both problems. "Four bedrooms, fenced yard, two blocks from the park" is fine.
  • In conversation. Do not characterize neighborhoods by who lives there. When a client asks "is this a good area?", give every client the same third-party sources on schools, crime statistics and amenities. Do not editorialize, and do not describe who lives there. Some brokerages have their own guidance on crime statistics, so confirm the wording with your broker before you share any source.
  • In service. Give every inquiry the same response time, the same information and the same options. Your speed-to-lead automation helps here because it treats everyone identically.

Texts and calls

The follow-up plans in Chapters 4 and 5 send automated texts. The rule behind them is simple: text only people who have told you they are happy to be texted.

  • Do: record where and when each person agreed to receive texts, in the contact's CRM record.
  • Do: stop all texts and automated plans the moment someone opts out, and log it.
  • Do not: add a contact to an automated text plan because you have their number.
  • Do not: call or text numbers on a do-not-call list, or outside the hours your state allows. Ask your broker for the current rules.

What to document

Keep notes in your CRM or your transaction system for every item below. Date, parties, what was said or delivered, and what was decided.

  • Agency disclosure given and signed, with the date.
  • Every offer, counteroffer and the client's instruction on each.
  • Pricing conversations and the client's decision.
  • Disclosures delivered (property condition, lead paint, HOA documents) and when.
  • Inspection findings discussed and the client's choices.
  • Any advice you gave that the client declined to follow.
  • Referrals to lenders, inspectors or attorneys, with a note that the client chose freely.

Professional standards

  • Follow the National Association of REALTORS® Code of Ethics and your local MLS rules.
  • Never give legal, tax or structural advice. Refer to the professional. Say: "That's a question for your attorney. I can introduce you to two."
  • Keep client information confidential, including after the transaction.
  • Respect do-not-call and anti-spam rules (see Texts and calls above). Honor every opt-out immediately in your CRM.
  • Know the advertising rules for your state. Marketing usually has to show your brokerage name and your license number. Ask your broker for the exact wording.
  • Do not pay or accept anything of value for referring a client to a lender, vendor or another agent until your broker has told you what is allowed.
  • Know your office policy on wire fraud warnings, and repeat the warning to every client before closing.
● SCRIPT · WIRE FRAUD WARNING
"Before closing, I want you to know that I will never email you wire instructions. If you get an email or text with wire instructions, even one that looks like it's from me or the title company, call me at the number I gave you before you send anything."
Purpose: protect the client from the most common closing-day fraud. Next step: log in the CRM that you gave the warning, with the date. Use the wording your broker or title company prefers.
● TRY IT TODAY
• Ask your broker for your state's agency disclosure form and read it aloud until you can explain it in two sentences.
• Review your last ten social posts and listing descriptions for language that describes people instead of property.
• Ask your broker what three situations they most want to hear about before you act.
• Open your Chapter 4 new-lead plan. Highlight every automated text. For each, write where consent was recorded.
• Practice the agency disclosure with a colleague as if they were a first-time buyer. They should ask two questions you must answer in plain words.
● CHECKPOINT
• Write down the moment your state requires agency disclosure, and where you found it.
• Name the seven federally protected classes, and one more that your state or city adds.
• What do you do when a client asks whether an area is "good"?

KEY TAKEAWAYS

  1. Disclose agency at first substantive contact and keep the signed copy.
  2. Describe property, never people. Treat every inquiry identically.
  3. Document decisions the same day. Ask your broker before anything unusual.
